Two effects pull in opposite directions. A low cap pays too little to cover pool slippage and gas near the threshold (see the unprofitable-skip counts below), so positions can drift under water before anyone closes them. A high cap reaches the "collateral cannot cover debt plus bonus" region at a smaller price drop and, once a position is under water, pays the liquidator more of the collateral that would otherwise repay suppliers. Above water the engine caps a closeout's bonus at the borrower's equity, so no cap turns a position with positive equity into a supplier loss.

Every liquidation is priced and applied with `risk-math`, the Rust port of the contract arithmetic. `test/vectors/HealthVectors.t.sol` checks it bit for bit against the engine on 100 shared vectors; those cover the liquidation state transition only (not interest accrual, the IRM or the oracle path, none of which the simulator models). Bad debt is what the engine writes off during closeouts plus, at the horizon, the shortfall of positions nobody liquidated. p99 is the nearest-rank percentile of the per-path bad debt, so with 400 paths it is the fifth-worst path.

## Limitations

- Stress calibration, not a forecast: high volatility and downward jumps that are not drift-compensated.
- Borrowers are passive (no top-ups or deleveraging), which overstates liquidations.
- One pool with constant depth; real depth thins out in crashes, which would understate cascades.
- Interest is not accrued over the one-day horizon.
- Liquidators are a single competitive population with fixed latency and gas; no MEV auctions or priority-fee wars.
- The oracle is the pool mid price; no oracle lag or deviation threshold.
- The grid is coarse (six caps, five LLTVs); the optimum between grid points is not resolved.
